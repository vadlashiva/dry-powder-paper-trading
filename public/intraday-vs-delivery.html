Intraday vs delivery trading: differences, charges and risk

Buy and sell the same day, or buy and hold? Intraday and delivery trades follow different rules for charges, margin and tax, and suit very different temperaments.

Updated 31 October 2026

The basic difference

Delivery means you buy shares and they're credited to your demat account; you can hold them for days or years. Intraday means you buy and sell (or sell and buy back) within the same trading day; nothing is delivered to your demat account.

Why intraday is harder than it looks

  • Leverage cuts both ways. With 5× leverage, a 2% move against you costs 10% of the money you put up.
  • Costs add up. Brokerage and charges are paid on every round trip; ten trades a day can eat most small gains.
  • Noise. Short-term price moves are mostly random. Edges are small and require discipline to capture.
  • Forced exit. Brokers square off intraday positions before the close (often around 3:15–3:25 PM), whether or not your trade has played out.

Which should a beginner choose?

Most beginners learn more, and lose less, by starting with delivery or short swing trades: there's time to think, charges are lower, and a stop-loss can be placed based on a daily chart. If you want to try intraday, practise it first with virtual money and include charges, so you see your true results.

Common questions

Is intraday trading better than delivery?

Neither is better in general. Intraday needs more time, discipline and capital efficiency; delivery suits people who can't watch the screen all day and want lower costs.

What happens if I don't close an intraday position?

Your broker automatically squares it off near the end of the day, often around 3:15–3:25 PM, and may charge a fee for doing so.

Are intraday profits taxed differently?

Yes. Intraday equity profits are treated as speculative business income and taxed at your slab rate, while delivery profits are capital gains. Check current rules or ask a tax adviser.
