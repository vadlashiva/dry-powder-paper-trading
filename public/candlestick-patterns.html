Candlestick patterns for beginners: 8 patterns worth knowing

Candlestick charts pack a lot of information into each bar. Once you can read a single candle, a handful of patterns help you spot moments when buyers or sellers may be losing control.

Updated 31 October 2026

Reading one candle

Each candle covers one period — a minute, an hour or a day. The body spans the open and close: green (or hollow) if it closed higher, red if lower. The thin wicks show the highest and lowest prices traded in that period. A long lower wick means sellers pushed the price down but buyers pushed it back up.

Single-candle patterns

1. Doji

Open and close are almost the same, leaving a tiny body. It signals indecision. After a strong trend it can be an early hint that momentum is fading.

2. Hammer

A small body near the top with a long lower wick, appearing after a fall. Sellers drove the price down, but buyers took it back. It suggests a possible bounce.

3. Inverted hammer

A small body near the bottom with a long upper wick, after a fall. Buyers tried to push higher; if the next candle confirms, it can mark a turning point.

4. Shooting star

The same shape as an inverted hammer but appearing after a rise. Buyers pushed up and were rejected — a warning sign for an uptrend.

Multi-candle patterns

5. Bullish engulfing

A red candle followed by a larger green candle whose body completely covers it. Buyers overwhelmed the previous period's sellers.

6. Bearish engulfing

The reverse: a green candle swallowed by a larger red one, often near a top.

7. Morning star

Three candles after a fall: a long red candle, a small indecisive candle, then a strong green candle closing well into the first one's body. A classic bottoming pattern.

8. Evening star

The mirror image at a top: long green, small candle, then a strong red candle.

How to use patterns without fooling yourself

  • Context matters more than shape. A hammer at a well-known support level means far more than one in the middle of nowhere.
  • Wait for confirmation. Many traders act only if the next candle moves in the expected direction.
  • Use a stop-loss. Patterns fail often. Place your stop beyond the pattern's high or low.
  • Check the timeframe. Daily-chart patterns tend to be more reliable than 1-minute ones, which are full of noise.

Common questions

Which candlestick pattern is the most reliable?

No pattern works on its own. Engulfing patterns and hammers at clear support or resistance levels, confirmed by the next candle, are among the more widely used.

What timeframe is best for candlestick patterns?

Higher timeframes like daily charts give fewer but more meaningful signals. Very short timeframes produce many patterns that fail.

Can I trade using only candlestick patterns?

It's risky. Most traders combine patterns with trend, support and resistance, volume and a strict stop-loss.
