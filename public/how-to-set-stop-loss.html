How to set a stop-loss: a beginner's guide with rupee examples

A stop-loss is the price at which you admit a trade isn't working and get out. Deciding it before you buy is the single habit that separates people who survive their first year of trading from those who don't.

Updated 31 October 2026

What a stop-loss actually does

When you buy a stock, you are making a guess about where its price will go. A stop-loss is a standing instruction to sell if the price falls to a level you choose in advance. It turns an open-ended loss ("I'll wait until it comes back") into a small, known one.

On most Indian brokers you place it as a stop-loss order: you set a trigger price, and when the stock trades at or below it, your sell order is sent to the exchange. PaperRally works the same way: tick Add a stop-loss when buying on the order ticket and the stop order is placed for you.

Three simple ways to choose the price

1. A fixed percentage

The easiest starting point: put the stop 1–3% below your entry. It's crude, because a 2% move is normal for a volatile stock and large for a sleepy one, but it's far better than no stop at all.

2. Below a recent swing low

Look at the chart for the most recent low the price bounced from. If your idea is "this stock is going up", a fall below that low means the idea is wrong. Place the stop a little under it, so normal noise doesn't trigger it.

3. Based on volatility (ATR)

The Average True Range shows how much a stock typically moves in a day. A stop of 1.5–2× ATR below entry gives the trade room to breathe in proportion to how jumpy the stock is. PaperRally's Indicators tab shows ATR for any stock.

Turn the stop into a rupee amount

The distance between your entry and your stop, multiplied by the number of shares, is the most you plan to lose. Work it out before you click buy.

Notice the target: aiming to make at least twice what you risk means you can be wrong more often than right and still come out ahead.

Mistakes that make stop-losses fail

  • Moving it further away when the price gets close. This is the most expensive habit in trading. Move a stop up to lock in profit, never down.
  • Putting it exactly on a round number or an obvious low. Many traders do the same, so prices often dip just through those levels. Leave a small buffer.
  • Making it too tight. A stop inside normal daily noise gets hit constantly. If the right stop is far away, buy fewer shares instead.
  • Not having one "for now". Decide the stop before entry, while you're calm.
Gaps are the exception: if a stock opens far below your stop after bad news, a stop-loss order fills at the next available price, which can be worse than your stop. That's one more reason to keep position sizes sensible.

Common questions

What is a good stop-loss percentage?

There is no single right number. Many beginners start with 1–3% below entry for large, liquid stocks, then move to stops based on chart levels or ATR. More important is limiting the rupee loss per trade to about 1–2% of your capital.

Should I move my stop-loss?

Only in the direction of profit. Raising a stop to lock in gains is fine; lowering it to avoid being stopped out turns a small loss into a big one.

What is the difference between SL and SL-M orders?

An SL (stop-limit) order becomes a limit order at your price when triggered, so it may not fill in a fast fall. An SL-M (stop-market) order becomes a market order and fills at the next available price.
